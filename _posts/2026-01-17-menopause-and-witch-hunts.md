The image of the witch is often an old woman: isolated, sharp-featured, living at the edge of a village and possessing knowledge that other people fear.

The image is familiar, but it can obscure the historical record.

Women were the majority of those accused of witchcraft in many parts of early modern Europe. Older women and widows appear in a number of regional studies, but the accused were not all elderly. They were not all unmarried, poor or socially isolated. Nor do trial records generally tell us whether a woman had reached menopause.

The distinction matters. Menopause and witchcraft have become closely associated in modern interpretations of the witch figure, but the historical relationship is much less direct.

To understand who was accused, we need to look at the conditions in which accusations developed: local conflict, reputation, household relationships, economic circumstances, religious belief and the legal systems that turned suspicion into prosecution.

## What the records can and cannot tell us

Witch-trial records are not neutral descriptions of everyday life. They were produced through legal procedures, interrogations, denunciations and, in some cases, torture.

They tell us what authorities and accusers claimed, what questions investigators asked and how cases developed. They do not necessarily provide an accurate account of the accused person's life or the events that supposedly took place.

Age can sometimes be established from a record or reconstructed from other evidence. Menopausal status is much harder to determine. A woman described as old, elderly or past childbearing age cannot automatically be classified as menopausal.

This is why a universal claim that the typical accused woman was between 45 and 65 is misleading unless it is tied to a clearly defined dataset, region and period.

European witch persecutions took place across different legal and political systems over several centuries. The profile of an accused person in one town cannot stand for every region.

## Who was accused?

Women formed the majority of accused people in many European witch-trial settings, although the proportion varied considerably by region.

The accused were not a single social type. They could be married or widowed, young or old, wealthy or poor. Some were already known within their communities because of disputes, unusual reputations or earlier allegations. Others became involved in cases through the accusations of neighbours or relatives.

The age of accused women also varied. Research into early modern Rothenburg, for example, shows that accused women could range from childhood to advanced old age. Many were married, while others were widows.

This kind of regional evidence is more useful than an imagined average age for all European witch trials. It shows that the historical pattern was not limited to elderly women.

## Neighbourhood conflict and the making of an accusation

Many accusations developed within ordinary relationships.

A disagreement over money, food, animals, land or assistance could become part of a later accusation. A person who was already disliked or feared might be blamed when illness, injury, death or misfortune occurred.

The accusation could follow a pattern:

- A conflict or misfortune took place.
- Someone remembered an earlier disagreement or threat.
- Suspicion attached itself to a particular person.
- A reputation or previous allegation made the suspicion appear credible.
- Neighbours or authorities repeated and developed the claim.

This does not mean every case began with a simple quarrel. Religious beliefs, political circumstances, local authorities and wider witchcraft panics could shape the process. But it does remind us that accusations often grew out of specific relationships rather than from a general fear of old women.

## Widowhood, poverty and social position

Widowhood could change a woman's position within a household and community.

A widow might lose the protection or income associated with a husband. Her access to property could depend on inheritance arrangements, family relationships and local law. Some widows were economically secure; others were vulnerable.

Living alone did not automatically make a woman a target. Nor were all poor women accused of witchcraft. But social circumstances could influence how a person was perceived and how effectively she could defend herself.

A woman with limited family support might have fewer people willing to speak on her behalf. A long-standing dispute could become more dangerous when a person lacked the resources or relationships to resist an allegation.

These conditions help explain vulnerability. They do not establish that widowhood or age directly caused accusation.

## Healers, midwives and women's knowledge

The familiar story of the herbalist or midwife persecuted simply for knowing too much is appealing, but it is not a reliable summary of the historical evidence.

Women did carry practical knowledge about childbirth, plants, illness and household care. Some people consulted healers or charmers. But the existence of women's healing knowledge does not mean that healers as a group were routinely prosecuted as witches.

The categories were not interchangeable. A midwife was not automatically a folk healer. A folk healer was not necessarily a ritual specialist. A woman who knew remedies was not necessarily involved in practices that her neighbours considered suspicious.

In particular cases, a person's reputation, relationships or alleged actions could become part of an accusation. But the claim that European witch hunts were primarily a campaign against midwives or herbalists requires evidence that cannot be supplied by repeating the familiar image of the wise woman.

The historical record is more varied, and less romantic, than that story suggests.

## Was menopause connected to witchcraft accusations?

There is no sound basis for saying that menopause itself made women more likely to be accused.

The records generally do not document whether a woman was menstruating, menopausal or post-menopausal. Even when an accused person was described as elderly, that description does not establish her reproductive status.

It is possible to explore the cultural symbolism of ageing, fertility and women's bodies. It is also possible to examine how older women were represented in witchcraft stories. But these questions should not be confused with the causes of historical prosecutions.

Menopause is a biological transition. Witchcraft accusation was a social and legal process shaped by beliefs, relationships and institutions. A connection between the two must be demonstrated in specific evidence, not inferred from the fact that some accused women were older.

## The old woman in the imagination of the witch

The image of the old witch survived long after the major European witch persecutions.

Folktales, literature, theatre and later popular culture repeatedly returned to the older woman who knew too much, lived apart from the community or possessed knowledge beyond ordinary understanding.

These images are important, but they belong to the history of representation. They do not provide a demographic profile of the people accused in court.

A story can preserve anxieties about age, independence, women's knowledge and social boundaries. A trial record can reveal the legal and social process through which a particular person was accused. Reading the two together can be illuminating, but they must remain distinct forms of evidence.

## What the historical pattern really shows

The European witch hunts cannot be explained by menopause, old age or women's herbal knowledge alone.

Women were accused in large numbers in many regions, but their ages, household positions and circumstances varied. Some were widows, some were married, and some were young. Some had difficult relationships with neighbours; others became involved in wider waves of accusation.

The most defensible conclusion is not that the witch hunts targeted menopausal women. It is that accusations developed within particular social and institutional settings, and that women could be especially exposed to the consequences of those settings.

The old witch remains a powerful figure in folklore. But the women named in trial records were not archetypes. They were individuals living in communities where fear, conflict and legal authority could turn suspicion into prosecution.

## FAQ

### Were most women accused of witchcraft menopausal?

The surviving trial records rarely establish whether an accused woman was menopausal. Age cannot be used as a reliable substitute for reproductive status.

### Were older women more likely to be accused of witchcraft?

Older women appear in many regional records and historical studies, but patterns differed between jurisdictions. Age alone does not explain why a person was accused.

### Were widows especially vulnerable?

Widowhood could affect a woman's household position and economic security, but widows were not the only women accused. Local relationships, reputation and conflict also mattered.

### Were herbalists and midwives commonly prosecuted as witches?

It is misleading to assume that healers and midwives were routinely targeted because of their knowledge. The relationship between healing, reputation and accusation varied by place and case.

### Did menopause cause the European witch hunts?

There is no historical evidence that menopause caused the witch hunts. The persecutions developed through particular legal, religious and social conditions.
