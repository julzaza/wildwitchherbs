There is a particular figure who appears repeatedly in European folklore: the older woman who lives at the edge of the village, knows the plants, remembers old customs and understands things that younger people have forgotten.

Sometimes she is sought out.

Sometimes she is accused of knowing too much.

She is the wise woman, the healer, the widow, the cunning woman and, in darker stories, the witch.

It is tempting to connect this figure directly with menopause, imagining that the end of menstruation marked a formal passage into supernatural authority. The historical evidence, however, is more complicated.

Menopause was rarely treated as a distinct cultural event in the way modern society might recognize it. The cessation of menstruation belonged to a wider transition of ageing, changing family responsibilities and altered social expectations.

Yet the older woman occupied a particular place in the European imagination. She had lived beyond the years of childbirth. She had accumulated experience, witnessed generations come and go, and often possessed knowledge that was transmitted outside formal institutions.

Her position could be powerful, but it was not necessarily secure.

To understand the folklore surrounding older women, we must look beyond the romantic image of the wise crone and examine the uncomfortable relationship between knowledge, gender, age and fear.

## 1. Menopause Before It Became a Medical Category

The word *menopause* is relatively modern.

Although ancient and medieval medical writers discussed menstruation, fertility and ageing, they did not possess the same clearly defined medical category that exists today.

In early European medical writing, the end of menstruation was generally considered within broader discussions of ageing and reproductive capacity. It was not consistently treated as a separate condition requiring its own medical explanation.

Historical research by medical historian Alison M. Downham Moore shows that the cessation of menstruation received relatively little independent medical attention in early modern Europe. More substantial medical discussions emerged gradually, particularly during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

The word itself entered French medical writing in the early nineteenth century.

This distinction matters.

Women did not suddenly begin experiencing menopause when physicians named it. What changed was the language through which their bodies were interpreted.

For generations, the end of menstruation had existed as one part of a woman's changing life. It was not necessarily understood as a crisis, a liberation or a spiritual awakening.

It was a bodily transition whose meaning depended heavily on the society in which a woman lived.

## 2. The Older Woman: Knowledge, Authority and Social Position

In pre-industrial European communities, knowledge was not always held by those with formal education.

Much of women's knowledge was practical and transmitted through families and communities.

It included:

- The preparation and use of medicinal plants
- Knowledge of pregnancy, childbirth and infant care
- Food preservation and household management
- Care of the sick and dying
- The observance of seasonal customs
- Protective practices and local religious traditions

Older women could become important custodians of this knowledge.

A woman who had raised children, cared for relatives and lived through difficult seasons might possess decades of experience. Her knowledge could be valuable to younger women, neighbours and extended family.

But age did not automatically confer authority.

A wealthy widow with land and supportive relatives occupied a very different position from a poor woman living alone without family protection.

A woman respected within her household might be vulnerable outside it.

The social position of older women depended on property, inheritance, marital status, family relationships and economic security.

This is important when examining the folklore of the wise woman. Her authority was not simply a gift bestowed by age or the end of menstruation. It was also shaped by the practical structures of the community around her.

## 4. The Wise Woman and the Feared Witch

The older woman is one of the most recognizable figures in European witchcraft imagery.

But the historical relationship between older women and witch accusations deserves careful attention.

During the European witch persecutions, women formed the majority of those accused in many regions. Older women, widows and women living outside conventional household structures appear frequently in surviving records.

Yet the accused were not exclusively elderly.

The age and circumstances of accused women varied considerably between communities. Research into individual witchcraft trials shows that women of different ages could become involved in accusations, investigations and prosecutions.

In early modern Rothenburg, for example, women accused of witchcraft ranged from childhood to advanced old age. Many were married, while others were widows.

Age alone does not explain why someone became a target.

Accusations could emerge from:

- Disputes between neighbours
- Suspicions following illness or death
- Conflicts involving property or inheritance
- Economic hardship
- Reputation and existing allegations
- Religious and political tensions
- Long-standing personal hostility

Older women could be vulnerable when they lacked strong family or economic support. But this was a social vulnerability, not a consequence of menopause itself.

There is another important distinction.

The folk healer and the accused witch were not necessarily the same person. Nor did every woman who practised herbal medicine face suspicion.

Nevertheless, the cultural imagination could transform familiar knowledge into something threatening.

The woman who knew the remedies was also imagined as someone who might know the poison.

The woman who understood protective charms might be suspected of knowing how to remove protection.

In this way, the figure of the witch reflected wider anxieties about women's knowledge, independence and perceived influence.

## 5. Blood, Fertility and the Symbolism of Transformation

Menstruation has carried powerful symbolic meanings in many religious and folk traditions.

Blood could represent fertility, vitality, kinship, danger, purification or ritual restriction. Its meaning depended on the particular culture and religious framework.

The end of menstruation therefore had potential symbolic significance, but we should not assume that every tradition interpreted it in the same way.

Some religious systems attached restrictions to menstrual blood. Others incorporated blood into ideas about fertility, protection and the sacred.

In medieval Christian Europe, for example, menstrual blood was sometimes discussed through theological ideas about purity and the biblical Fall. Such ideas were not identical across all communities, and they should not be confused with a universal European folk belief.

Nor is there sufficient evidence to claim that post-menopausal women were generally believed to be protected from spirits because they no longer bled.

That is an interesting symbolic interpretation, but it cannot be presented as a general historical fact.

What we can recognize is that the cessation of menstruation marked the end of one reproductive phase of life.

It changed a woman's relationship to pregnancy and fertility. It did not erase her sexuality, her identity, her knowledge or her place in the community.

The symbolism of that transition was never fixed.

## 6. The Crone: Historical Figure or Modern Invention?

The crone has become one of the most familiar figures in modern witchcraft and contemporary pagan spirituality.

She is often presented as the third stage of the Triple Goddess:

- Maiden
- Mother
- Crone

In this framework, the crone represents wisdom, death, transformation, intuition and the power of old age.

It is an evocative image.

But it is important to distinguish the modern spiritual archetype from historical European life.

The idea that every traditional European culture recognized a universal Maiden-Mother-Crone system is not historically established.

The familiar modern framework owes much to twentieth-century pagan and feminist interpretations of older mythological material.

Historical older women were not universally celebrated as wise elders.

Some were respected. Some were economically secure. Some were loved and influential within their families. Others experienced poverty, exclusion, illness and isolation.

The romantic image of the crone can conceal these realities.

It can also overlook something important: an older woman did not need to become supernatural, maternal or spiritually useful to deserve respect.

She was a person whose life continued beyond childbirth.

Her authority, where it existed, was not necessarily granted by a goddess or a ritual title. It could come from experience, relationships, knowledge and the simple fact of having survived.

## 7. When Menopause Became a Medical Problem

The history of modern menopause is more complicated than the familiar claim that ancient medicine always regarded it as a disease.

Medical historian Alison M. Downham Moore's research challenges this assumption.

In early modern European medical writing, the final cessation of menstruation was often treated as a natural consequence of ageing. It received relatively little independent attention compared with menstrual disorders in younger women.

The situation changed during the eighteenth and particularly the nineteenth century.

French physicians developed a more distinct medical concept of menopause, with increasing attention to its symptoms and management.

The body of medical writing expanded, and the transition became a recognized subject of medical intervention.

This history does not mean that menopause symptoms were invented by doctors.

Hot flashes, sleep disturbances, changes in mood and other physical experiences are real. Women experienced difficulties long before modern medicine developed its terminology.

Nor does the existence of medical treatment mean that menopause should be understood only as a disorder.

There is a difference between recognizing symptoms that deserve care and treating the entire transition as a failure of the female body.

Modern medicine can offer valuable support, and hormone therapy or other treatments may be appropriate for some women. Decisions should be based on individual circumstances and medical advice.

But medical language is not the only language through which women can understand ageing.

Cultural history, family knowledge, traditional practices and personal experience also shape how this transition is lived.

The important question is not whether medicine is wrong, but whether a medical explanation is sufficient to describe an entire stage of human life.

## The Wider Cycle: Menstruation, Fertility and Women's Folk Knowledge

Menopause is only one part of a much longer relationship between women, their bodies and the natural world.

Across European folk traditions, menstruation, fertility, pregnancy and the transition into older age were surrounded by customs, plant knowledge and ritual practices. These were not isolated subjects, but interconnected aspects of women's lives.

Menstrual blood carried meanings of fertility, vulnerability and transformation. Plants were gathered for women's health, protective customs accompanied important life transitions, and fertility was sometimes approached through offerings, prayers and household rituals.

## 8. What the Old Stories Leave Behind

The older woman in European folklore is not an uncomplicated symbol of liberation.

She is not always powerful, always feared or always revered.

She exists in the tension between these possibilities.

She can be the woman called upon when a child is ill, the neighbour who knows the old remedies, the widow who remembers another generation, or the isolated woman whose independence becomes a source of suspicion.

Her knowledge can make her valuable.

It can also make her uncomfortable to those who would rather see women remain within familiar boundaries.

The modern crone archetype captures something of this history, but it should not be mistaken for the whole of it.

Perhaps the most interesting part of the story is not that older women were believed to possess extraordinary powers.

It is that their lives continued after the roles traditionally assigned to them had changed.

They did not disappear when their reproductive years ended.

They remained healers, mothers, grandmothers, neighbours, workers, storytellers and sometimes women who simply wanted to be left alone.

Menopause did not make a woman a witch.

It did not automatically make her wise.

It did not remove her from the world of desire, work, relationships or ordinary human struggle.

But the older woman has long occupied a complicated place in the stories communities tell about power, knowledge and the unknown.

And perhaps that is why she continues to appear at the edge of the village, in the forest, beside the hearth and in the imagination of the witch.

Not because she belongs to another world.

But because she has lived long enough to know this one.

## FAQ

### Did traditional cultures celebrate menopause?

There was no universal celebration of menopause. The end of menstruation was generally part of the wider experience of ageing, and the social meaning varied between cultures and communities.

### Were menopausal women considered witches?

Some older women were associated with witchcraft in European folklore and historical accusations. However, menopause itself was not a universal explanation for witchcraft accusations.

### Did older women have special roles in Balkan folk medicine?

Women transmitted substantial practical knowledge, including remedies, household practices and protective customs. However, these roles were not exclusive to menopausal women and varied between regions.

### Was menopause considered an illness in ancient Europe?

Not in the same distinct medical sense used today. Early medical writers discussed ageing and the cessation of menstruation, but menopause became a more defined medical subject much later.

### Is the crone archetype historically accurate?

It draws on genuine cultural associations between older women, knowledge and supernatural power. However, the universal Maiden-Mother-Crone framework is largely a modern spiritual interpretation rather than a documented universal ancient European tradition.

### Did menopause make women more spiritually powerful?

There is no historical evidence for a universal belief that menopause itself increased spiritual power. Some traditions associated older women with accumulated knowledge and supernatural influence, but interpretations differed.
