There is a familiar figure in European folklore: an older woman who remembers the old customs, knows the plants, understands the household remedies and has lived long enough to witness several generations.

Sometimes people seek her advice. Sometimes they distrust her. In stories, she may appear as a healer, a widow, a cunning woman, a prophetess or a witch.

It is tempting to connect this figure directly with menopause, as though the end of menstruation marked a recognised passage into supernatural authority. The historical evidence does not support such a simple explanation.

Menopause was not always treated as a distinct cultural event. The end of menstruation belonged to a wider experience of ageing, changing family responsibilities and altered expectations of women's lives. The older woman could occupy an important position, but her standing depended on much more than her reproductive age.

To understand the folklore surrounding her, we need to look beyond the romantic image of the wise crone and consider the relationship between age, knowledge, gender and social power.

## Menopause before it became a medical category

The word *menopause* is relatively modern. Ancient and medieval medical writers discussed menstruation, fertility and ageing, but they did not use the same clearly defined medical category that exists today.

Medical historian Alison M. Downham Moore has examined how the cessation of menstruation was treated in European medical writing. In early modern sources, it often appeared within broader discussions of ageing and reproductive capacity, rather than as an independent subject receiving sustained attention.

Medical discussion developed over time, particularly during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The word itself entered French medical writing in the early nineteenth century.

This distinction matters. Women did not begin experiencing menopause when physicians named it. What changed was the language through which their bodies were described and interpreted.

The end of menstruation could be understood as one part of a woman's changing life. It was not necessarily considered a crisis, a liberation or a spiritual awakening. Its meaning depended on the medical, religious and social ideas of the community in which she lived.

## The older woman: knowledge and social position

In pre-industrial communities, practical knowledge was often transmitted through families and local relationships rather than formal institutions.

Women's knowledge could include:

- The preparation and use of medicinal plants.
- Pregnancy, childbirth and infant care.
- Food preservation and household management.
- Caring for the sick and dying.
- Seasonal customs and household observances.
- Protective practices and local religious traditions.

Older women could become important custodians of this knowledge. A woman who had raised children, cared for relatives and lived through difficult seasons might possess decades of experience.

But age did not automatically confer authority.

A widow with property and supportive relatives occupied a different position from a poor woman living alone. A woman respected within her household might have little protection in a dispute with neighbours or more powerful members of the community.

The social position of older women depended on property, inheritance, marital status, kinship and economic security. The image of the wise woman therefore tells only part of the story. Knowledge could make a woman useful and respected, but it did not guarantee her independence or safety.

## The older woman and the feared witch

Older women are prominent in many European representations of witchcraft. They also appear in records of witchcraft accusations, although the accused were not exclusively elderly.

The historical record does not justify a simple claim that menopausal women were targeted because they had stopped menstruating. Trial documents generally record accusations, relationships, reputations and alleged acts. They do not reliably establish whether an accused woman was menopausal.

The circumstances of accusation varied. Neighbour disputes, illness, death, property conflicts, economic hardship, existing reputations and religious or political tensions could all play a part.

Nor should the folk healer and the accused witch be treated as interchangeable figures. Women who knew plants, remedies or protective customs were not automatically suspected of witchcraft. The relationship between healing and accusation depended on local circumstances.

Nevertheless, stories about witches often expressed anxieties about influence that could not easily be controlled. A woman who was believed to know how to heal might also be imagined as knowing how to harm. A person who could perform a protective charm might be suspected of being able to undo protection.

These are cultural associations, not proof that herbal knowledge itself caused persecution.

## Menstruation, blood and the meaning of transition

Menstrual blood has carried different meanings across religious and folk traditions. Depending on the context, blood could be associated with fertility, kinship, danger, purification, ritual restriction or vitality.

It would be misleading to turn these varied beliefs into one European system. Religious teaching, local custom and household practice did not always agree.

In some Christian contexts, menstruation was discussed through ideas about ritual purity and sacred space. These discussions should not be confused with a universal folk belief shared by all European communities.

The end of menstruation could therefore have symbolic significance, but the evidence does not support the claim that post-menopausal women were generally believed to be protected from spirits because they no longer bled. That interpretation may be meaningful in a modern spiritual framework, but it should not be presented as an established historical fact.

The cessation of menstruation marked the end of one reproductive phase. It did not erase a woman's sexuality, identity, knowledge or place in her community.

## The crone: historical figure or modern archetype?

The crone has become a familiar figure in modern witchcraft and contemporary pagan spirituality. She is often presented as the third stage of the Triple Goddess:

- Maiden
- Mother
- Crone

In this framework, the crone represents wisdom, death, transformation, intuition and the power of old age.

It is an evocative image, but it should be distinguished from historical European life. The idea that every traditional European culture recognised a universal Maiden-Mother-Crone system is not historically established. The familiar framework owes much to modern pagan and feminist interpretations of older mythological material.

Historical older women were not universally celebrated as wise elders. Some were respected and economically secure. Others experienced poverty, illness, exclusion or isolation. Many lived ordinary lives that did not fit either the wise-woman or witch figure.

An older woman did not need to become supernatural, maternal or spiritually useful to deserve respect. Her authority, where it existed, might come from experience, relationships, work, property or knowledge.

The modern crone can be a meaningful symbol. It becomes misleading only when it is mistaken for a documented universal ancient tradition.

## When menopause became a medical problem

The history of modern menopause is more complicated than the claim that ancient medicine always regarded it as a disease.

Downham Moore's historical research challenges the assumption that menopause was consistently treated as an independent medical problem in earlier European writing. The subject became more prominent as medical discussion developed during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

This does not mean that symptoms were invented by doctors. Women experienced changes in sleep, temperature regulation, mood and other aspects of health long before modern medicine developed its terminology.

Nor does recognising the history of medicalisation mean that treatment is unnecessary. Medical care can be valuable, and decisions about hormone therapy or other treatments should be made according to individual circumstances and appropriate medical advice.

The distinction is between recognising symptoms that deserve care and treating the entire transition as a failure of the female body.

Medicine can explain important aspects of menopause. It cannot, by itself, describe the full cultural, social and personal experience of ageing.

## The wider cycle of women's knowledge

Menopause is one part of a longer history of women's bodies, work and knowledge.

Menstruation, fertility, pregnancy, childbirth and ageing were surrounded by customs and interpretations that differed between communities. Women's practical knowledge might include plants, food, care, household protection and the observance of important life events.

These subjects are connected, but they should not be collapsed into a single system of ancient female wisdom.

## What the old stories leave behind

The older woman in European folklore is not an uncomplicated symbol of liberation. She is not always powerful, feared or revered.

She might be the neighbour called upon when a child was ill, the woman who remembered a remedy, the widow who held family knowledge, or the isolated person whose independence became a source of suspicion.

Her knowledge could make her valuable. Her circumstances could also make her vulnerable.

Menopause did not make a woman a witch. It did not automatically make her wise, and it did not remove her from ordinary human life.

The older woman remained a worker, neighbour, relative, healer, storyteller or simply a person living beyond the years of childbirth. The stories communities told about her reveal as much about their fears and expectations as they do about the women themselves.

The most useful way to approach this history is not to search for one lost doctrine of female power. It is to recover the different lives, customs and beliefs that made older women visible in the first place.

## FAQ

### Did traditional European cultures celebrate menopause?

There was no single European tradition of celebrating menopause. The end of menstruation was generally understood within wider ideas about ageing, family and social position, which varied between communities.

### Were menopausal women considered witches?

There is no evidence that menopause itself was a universal explanation for witchcraft accusations. Some older women were accused, but the circumstances differed between places and cases.

### Did older women have special roles in folk medicine?

Women transmitted practical knowledge about plants, childbirth, food, care and household customs. These roles were shaped by family and community circumstances and were not exclusive to menopausal women.

### Was menopause considered an illness in ancient Europe?

Not in the same distinct medical sense used today. Earlier writers discussed ageing and the cessation of menstruation, while menopause became a more defined medical subject much later.

### Is the Maiden-Mother-Crone system an ancient European belief?

The familiar framework is a modern spiritual interpretation. It should not be treated as a universal system shared by all historical European cultures.

### Did menopause make women spiritually powerful?

There is no evidence for a universal historical belief that menopause itself granted spiritual power. Some traditions associated older women with accumulated knowledge or supernatural influence, but interpretations varied.
