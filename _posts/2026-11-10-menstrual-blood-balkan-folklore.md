Menstrual blood has occupied an uneasy place in the folklore of the Balkans.

It could be hidden, feared and treated as a source of danger. Yet it could also be preserved for use in magic, associated with fertility or believed to influence the affection and behaviour of another person.

This apparent contradiction is central to the subject. Menstrual blood was not always understood simply as something unclean. In some traditions, it was considered powerful precisely because it was surrounded by restrictions and uncertainty.

The evidence is regional. Serbian and Bulgarian ethnographic research preserves particular beliefs and customs, but there was no single Balkan system governing menstruation. Practices differed between communities, religious settings and generations.

The following account focuses especially on documented Serbian village traditions, with Bulgarian research providing a second regional perspective.

## A region of different customs

The Balkans are not one folklore culture. Serbian, Montenegrin, Bulgarian, Albanian, Macedonian, Romanian and other communities developed distinct customs concerning menstruation, fertility, the body and ritual purity.

Even within one country, practices could differ from village to village. A custom remembered by women in western Serbia cannot automatically be applied to Montenegro or Bulgaria. Nor should a village practice be treated as an official religious rule.

Two studies are particularly useful for understanding this subject.

In 1990, Miroslava Malešević published *Sexual Maturation of Girls: Puberty Rites in a Traditional Serbian Village*. Her research was based on interviews conducted in 72 Orthodox villages in Serbia. Many of the women interviewed were elderly and remembered the customs of their own youth.

Mila Bankova's 2022 study, *“It Should Be Recalled It's a Women's Job”: Sociocultural Parameters of Menstruation in Bulgarian Traditional Culture and Folklore*, examines menstrual beliefs, menarche rituals and folklore motifs in Bulgarian tradition.

Together, these studies show that menstruation could be understood through ideas of fertility, danger, social transition and the proper order of household and community life.

## The first blood: a girl's passage into womanhood

In the Serbian village traditions recorded by Malešević, the first menstruation was more than a private physical event.

It marked a change in a girl's social position. Before menarche, she belonged to the category of children. Afterward, she was recognised as sexually mature and potentially marriageable.

The transition was not always announced openly. A mother might be the first person told. Other women in the household gradually became aware of the change, while the family could behave as though nothing unusual had happened.

The first menstruation could also be marked by customs and restrictions. The girl was expected to follow particular rules while bleeding, and her return to ordinary activities could involve washing and other ritual actions.

The first major public appearance after the event was significant. Malešević describes a girl being accompanied by her mother to a holiday gathering, dressed and presented as a young woman. She could now participate in dances and social activities associated with marriageable girls.

The transition therefore had two dimensions: a bodily change and a change in social identity.

It also reveals the importance attached to female fertility. In a society where marriage and children were central to family continuity, the first menstruation signalled the beginning of a new stage in a girl's expected life.

## Menstrual blood: dangerous and powerful

The accounts collected by Malešević reveal an ambivalent attitude towards menstrual blood.

A menstruating girl could be considered vulnerable to harmful magic. Her blood and stained clothing were not supposed to be exposed, because another person might use them to bewitch her or cause harm.

At the same time, menstrual blood was believed to possess magical potential.

The cloth stained during a girl's first menstruation could be kept secretly. In the beliefs recorded by Malešević, it might later be used in love magic. A girl who wished to attract a particular young man could use the stained cloth in a ritual intended to make him desire her.

The study also records a belief that the blood of the first menstruation could protect a soldier from bullets. A piece of stained cloth might be sewn into the uniform of a brother or husband without his knowledge.

These examples are striking because the blood was not treated as powerless or merely contaminating. It was considered capable of influencing events beyond the body from which it came.

The distinction between ordinary menstrual blood and the blood of menarche is important here. Some of the beliefs described in the study relate specifically to the first menstruation and the cloth associated with it. They should not be presented as universal beliefs about every menstrual cycle.

## Love magic and the desire to choose

The use of menstrual blood in love magic is especially revealing.

In the Serbian accounts, a girl could preserve the cloth from her first menstruation as a means of attracting a man she desired. The belief that the blood could influence his affection offered a way to express personal choice in a society where marriage decisions were often shaped by the family.

Malešević places these practices within the social organisation of traditional marriage.

A girl's future husband might be selected through family negotiations. Her own wishes could be subordinated to the interests of the household and the wider family community.

Love magic offered an imagined means of influencing the outcome without openly challenging those arrangements. The ritual could provide a sense of agency where direct choice was limited.

This does not mean that every girl performed such a practice or that the belief was universal. It does show how magical thinking could intersect with the practical constraints of young women's lives.

The blood was imagined as a means of influence, but the social context explains why that influence might have mattered.

## Restrictions during menstruation

The same research records a series of restrictions placed on menstruating girls and women.

During menstruation, activities outside the house could be reduced. A girl might be forbidden from entering the garden, feeding livestock with salt, making cheese or handling certain foods.

The reasons were specific.

Working in the garden while menstruating was believed to cause plants, fruit and vegetables to be damaged by a disease known as *plamenjača*. Feeding cattle with salt was believed to expose the animals to wolves. Making cheese or handling certain white fats was thought to cause red stains.

Entering church could also be forbidden because it was considered a desecration of a sacred place.

These rules were not simply a general instruction to avoid contact with other people. They connected menstrual blood with the household's productive life: gardens, livestock, food preparation and religious space.

A menstruating woman could be considered vulnerable to harmful influence, while her presence could also be believed to endanger people, animals, food or places.

The restrictions therefore operated in two directions. They were understood as protecting the community from the perceived effects of menstrual blood, while also protecting the woman from magical harm.

This double meaning is important. The same woman could be regarded as vulnerable and dangerous at the same time.

## Washing, cleansing and the return to ordinary life

In the customs recorded by Malešević, the end of bleeding also marked the end of the temporary restrictions.

The girl was expected to wash in flowing water. The washed laundry was left outside the house, on the branch of a fertile tree. After this, she was considered clean and could return to the house and her usual activities.

The details matter. The ritual did not simply involve washing the body. It also involved the handling and placement of the stained clothing, the use of flowing water and the association with a fertile tree.

The end of menstruation restored the girl's ordinary status within the household.

The cycle of restriction and return was repeated during later menstruations. The temporary state of impurity did not permanently define the woman. It was associated with the period of bleeding and ended when the prescribed practices had been completed.

Malešević's account describes an ambivalent system: the woman was treated as both vulnerable and potentially harmful, and the restrictions were intended to protect both her and the surrounding community.

These are historical folk beliefs, not medical facts. They should be understood within the ritual world in which they were recorded.

## Menstruation, Christianity and sacred space

Menstrual restrictions also intersected with religious practice.

In the Orthodox Serbian villages described by Malešević, menstruating girls could be prohibited from entering church. This was understood through the idea of ritual impurity and the perceived danger of menstrual blood entering a sacred space.

It is important to distinguish local custom from official religious teaching. Orthodox Christian attitudes and practices concerning menstruation have varied across time and place. A rule observed in one village should not be treated as a universal Orthodox position.

Bulgarian research provides another perspective. Mila Bankova's 2022 study examines menstruation in traditional Bulgarian culture and folklore, including menarche rituals and legends about the creation of the womb and menstruation.

Her analysis places women's reproductive cycles within a wider symbolic order. Menstruation and the womb are connected with fertility and the relationship between human life, the natural world and the sacred.

This is a different emphasis from the Serbian accounts of restriction and concealment. It reminds us that menstrual blood could be understood through more than one symbolic framework.

The same bodily process could be associated with danger and ritual boundaries in one context, and with fertility, cosmic order and the continuity of life in another.

## The contradiction at the centre of the tradition

The beliefs recorded in Serbian village life are not easy to reduce to a single idea of impurity.

Menstrual blood could be hidden because it was feared. A girl might be restricted from certain activities because she was believed to endanger the household or be vulnerable to magic. Yet the blood could also be preserved because it was thought to possess the power to attract love or protect a soldier.

This is not necessarily a contradiction within the logic of the tradition.

Something believed to possess power could also be considered dangerous. Its use might require secrecy, ritual handling or particular circumstances.

The restrictions also reveal the social position of women. Menstruation could interrupt ordinary work and place limits on movement, food preparation and participation in religious life. The beliefs therefore had practical consequences, particularly for girls and women whose labour was essential to the household.

At the same time, the use of menstrual blood in love magic suggests that women could imagine ways of influencing their lives through the very substance that placed them under restriction.

The tradition held both ideas together: female blood could be a source of vulnerability, and it could be a source of influence.

## What the folklore tells us

The historical accounts do not give us a single Balkan attitude towards menstruation. They reveal something more complex.

In particular Serbian village traditions, menstrual blood could mark a temporary state of ritual danger. The first menstruation could also announce a change in a girl's social status, while the cloth associated with it might be kept for magical purposes.

Bulgarian research records other symbolic dimensions, connecting menstruation and the womb with fertility, the organisation of the world and the sacred.

These beliefs should not be romanticised. They could place real restrictions on women's movements and work, and they reflected social expectations that were not equally applied to men.

Yet they also show that menstrual blood was rarely understood as an insignificant bodily substance. It occupied a powerful symbolic position, connected to the possibility of fertility, the vulnerability of the household, the influence of magic and the boundaries of sacred space.

The folklore preserves a world in which blood was not merely something a woman lost each month. It was something people interpreted, feared, concealed and sometimes sought to use.

## FAQ

### What did menstrual blood symbolise in Balkan folklore?

In documented Serbian and Bulgarian traditions, menstrual blood could be associated with fertility, ritual danger, vulnerability and magical influence. Its meaning varied between communities and practices.

### Was menstrual blood used in love magic?

Miroslava Malešević's research records Serbian beliefs in which cloth stained with a girl's first menstrual blood could be kept and later used in love magic. These accounts do not represent every Balkan tradition.

### Were menstruating women restricted from certain activities?

In the Serbian villages documented by Malešević, menstruating girls and women could be restricted from entering gardens, handling certain foods, feeding livestock with salt and entering church. Customs varied between communities.

### Was menstruation considered impure in Orthodox communities?

Some Orthodox Serbian village customs treated menstruation as a temporary state of ritual impurity. Local practice and religious interpretation were not identical everywhere.
